The Informational

One page of notes I wish someone had handed me before my first account.

Netiquette is the grammar of respect online.

Netiquette is the set of unwritten rules that keep a shared space usable. It is not politeness for its own sake. Typing in all capitals reads as shouting, sarcasm loses its tone on a screen, and a reply written in anger at midnight almost never improves with daylight. Most of what makes a platform ugly is not cruelty. It is carelessness at speed.

  • Read before you reply. Half of every argument online is two people answering a sentence nobody wrote.
  • Remember the audience is bigger than the addressee. A private joke can be read by a classmate who was not invited to laugh.
  • Do not feed a fight. You do not owe anyone a reply, especially at the moment you most want to send one.
  • Name the help you received. Gratitude in public is worth more than gratitude in a direct message.
  • Disagree with the idea, not the person. It is possible to be firm and still leave someone their dignity.

Your digital footprint has two halves: the one you build and the one others build for you.

The active footprint is what you choose to publish: posts, comments, uploads, the accounts you follow. The passive footprint is what gets collected around you: your location, your device, the websites you open at eleven at night, the photos other people tag you in, the comments you leave under a news page. You control the first with discipline. You control the second with settings, and with the company you keep.

Active

What you post, what you say, what you share. Presume it is permanent, printable, and searchable by a future employer.

Passive

What is collected while you use a service. Location history, cookies, device identifiers, and the tags other people add to your face.

Deleting is not erasing. A screenshot outlives the original post, and an archive outlives the screenshot. The only reliable protection is not publishing it in the first place.

Data privacy is a right, not a favour.

In the Philippines, Republic Act No. 10173, the Data Privacy Act of 2012, requires organisations to protect personal information and gives every person the right to be informed, to object, to access, and to correct it. That law is a floor, not a ceiling. Good sense sets a lower limit still: if you would not hand it to a stranger in the street, do not hand it to a form on the internet.

What I keep off a public profile:

  • My full home address and my daily schedule. A location is not a detail; it is an invitation if the wrong person reads it.
  • Live location and "I am home alone" posts. Post the photo when you have already left the building.
  • Birthdays paired with security answers. Mother's maiden name, first pet, first school. That is a password reset waiting to happen.
  • Photos of documents. School IDs, receipts, tickets, bank screens. The number is the point, and the photo is the leak.
  • Other people's children, plates, and faces. Their consent is not mine to give.

Before you share, run it through three gates: is it true, is it kind, is it necessary.

A claim does not become true because it is written clearly, and it does not become trustworthy because a friend reposted it. Screenshots are the easiest things in the world to make and the hardest to verify. The work of checking takes about ninety seconds, which is longer than most of us give, and shorter than the time it takes to apologise.

  1. Find the original. Not the screenshot, not the summary. The post, the report, the document itself, on the page where it was first published.
  2. Check the date. A great number of viral warnings are true, old, and already resolved.
  3. Look for a second voice. If a story is real, someone outside the first source is reporting the same facts.
  4. Ask who benefits. Outrage travels faster than correction because outrage is easier to sell.
  5. If you cannot verify it, do not forward it. Silence is not a lie, but a rumour with your name on it is.

Copyright is not a technicality.

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's work as your own, and it does not require intent. Copying a paragraph without a citation, submitting a downloaded essay, taking a photo from a search engine for a school project, all of it borrows without asking. Fair use is narrower than most students assume; it is a defence, not a permission, and it is decided case by case, by a court, after the fact. Credit is the simplest protection, and the least you can offer.

Cyberbullying is not banter.

Cyberbullying is repeated, deliberate harm carried out through a device: insults, threats, deliberate exclusion, spreading a rumour, running an account that pretends to be someone else. It is easier than bullying in a corridor because the screen hides the face, and it is worse because there is no corridor to walk out of. The phone follows its owner into the house, into the evening, into bed.

  • Do not reply. A reaction is the reward. Answering in anger gives them a second post to screenshot.
  • Keep the evidence. Screenshots with the date, the handle, and the thread intact.
  • Block and report. Use the platform tools, and recognise the pattern rather than the single message.
  • Tell one person. A teacher, a parent, a guidance counsellor, a friend. The hardest part is the first sentence, and it is also the part that ends it.
  • Check on the target. A message that says "I saw that, and it was not right" changes a person's whole night.
The bystander decides what happens next. Most of the harm online is not done by the person who posts. It is done by the people who watch, laugh, and scroll.
